The orchestrator chooses an agent by interpreting the resident’s message and matching it to the agent whose responsibilities, memory, and obligations are most relevant. It considers the intent of the request, the household context, and the type of task involved. For example, a scheduling request may go to the scheduling agent, a household preference question to the long-term memory agent, and a time-sensitive obligation to the reminder or task agent.

It also gathers the relevant context before responding, including short-term conversation history, long-term facts, and the current schedule of obligations. This helps the assistant avoid contradictions, duplicate reminders, or missed commitments. The orchestrator’s goal is not simply to produce the most interesting answer, but to route the message to the agent that can act on it correctly and consistently.

Reliability is emphasized over cleverness because household assistance depends on trust. A missed reminder, wrong appointment, or forgotten obligation can cause real inconvenience, even if the assistant’s response sounds helpful or sophisticated. In this setting, a plain, accurate, and timely response is better than a creative one that risks misunderstanding the resident’s needs.

This design also supports cooperative agents by keeping their roles clear and predictable. When each agent is responsible for a specific kind of memory or task, the system can maintain consistency across conversations and obligations. Reliability becomes the foundation for useful automation: the assistant must first be dependable, and only then can it be helpful in a more nuanced way.
