The orchestrator chooses which agent responds by first interpreting the resident’s message and identifying the task or intent behind it. It then checks the relevant context—such as short-term conversation history, long-term household facts, and scheduled obligations—to determine which agent is best suited to handle the request. For example, a scheduling question may go to the agent responsible for obligations, while a question about a household preference or past fact may go to the agent that maintains long-term memory.

The selection process is likely based on matching the message to the agent’s role, available context, and confidence in the answer. If multiple agents could respond, the orchestrator may prefer the one with the most direct access to the needed information or the one whose responsibility most closely aligns with the request. This keeps responses organized and reduces the chance of conflicting or incomplete answers.

Reliability is emphasized over cleverness because household assistance depends on trust and consistency. A missed reminder, incorrect schedule, or forgotten obligation can have real consequences, such as a resident missing an appointment or failing to complete an important task. In this setting, a simple but dependable response is more valuable than a creative or sophisticated one that might be wrong.
