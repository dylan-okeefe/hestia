The orchestrator chooses which agent responds by first interpreting the resident’s message and identifying the task type, such as a reminder, household coordination request, question about stored facts, or scheduling need. It then examines the relevant context: short-term memory for recent conversation, long-term facts for stable household information, and the schedule of obligations for time-sensitive commitments. Based on that context, it routes the message to the agent best suited to handle it, such as a reminder agent, scheduling agent, memory agent, or household coordination agent.

The selection process also considers confidence and overlap. If multiple agents could respond, the orchestrator prefers the one with the clearest match to the request and the most reliable access to the needed information. For example, a request like “remind me to take out the trash tomorrow” should go to the reminder or scheduling agent rather than a general conversational agent. If the intent is ambiguous, the orchestrator may ask for clarification or use a conservative fallback agent rather than guessing.

Reliability is emphasized over cleverness because household assistance depends on trust and predictability. A resident may rely on reminders, appointments, and task coordination for real-world obligations, so a missed reminder or incorrect schedule can cause practical harm. A clever but uncertain response may sound helpful, but if it fails to act or misinterprets the request, it undermines the system’s usefulness.

Therefore, the orchestrator favors clear routing, explicit context use, and conservative behavior when uncertain. Plain, dependable responses are preferred because they reduce the risk
